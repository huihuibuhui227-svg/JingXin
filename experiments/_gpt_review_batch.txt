## Overall assessment

The abstract is strongest where it reports the experimental setup and the negative diagnostic result:

- RecruitView evaluation: **supported**
- Broad baseline coverage: **supported**
- All LLM methods negatively correlated with human ratings: confirmed by Table 1
- Feature-shuffling invariance: **supported**
- \(\Delta r=0.009\): **supported**

Its major overclaims concern the interpretation of generated CoT:

- The data do **not** show an effective bridge from behavioral cues to LLM reasoning.
- The data do **not** establish faithful psychological-state inference.
- There is no human evaluation supporting transparency or auditability.
- “Fully inspectable” is untenable.
- Inter-model \(r=0.742\) demonstrates agreement, not reasoning robustness.
- CueCoT is not significantly different from direct prompting (\(p=0.085\)).
- CueCoT’s macro correlation is negative, \(r=-0.1283\), while the best conventional model, RF(All), reaches \(r=0.3829\).

Thus, the abstract accurately discloses the strongest failure result, but its earlier claims about grounding, reasoning, transparency, stage-wise contribution, and robustness are substantially stronger than the experimental evidence permits.

ROUND 2: Methodology & Experiments
============================================================
## 1. “Structured soft prompts” vs. natural-language descriptions

Yes, this is a substantive terminology/method discrepancy.

A **soft prompt** normally means a sequence of continuous, often trainable embedding vectors inserted into the LLM’s embedding space. Percentile-based descriptions serialized into 300–500 natural-language tokens are **discrete textual or hard prompts**, not soft prompts. A deterministic feature-to-text serializer also is not conventionally a “projection into token space.”

The paper should do one of the following:

- Revise the abstract and method to say that features are **quantized/serialized into structured natural-language prompts**; or
- Actually implement a learned projector that maps numerical features to continuous LLM embeddings, and explain how it is trained and inserted into the model.

The method also needs to specify:

- How percentile thresholds are estimated;
- Whether thresholds and feature normalization use **training users only**;
- How temporal windows are aggregated into 300–500 tokens;
- How missing faces, hands, audio, or unreliable measurements are represented;
- Whether serialization is deterministic across all LLM variants.

Estimating percentiles globally would leak test-set distributional information.

---

## 2. User-level five-seed split and evaluating GPT-5.6 on all videos

### User-level split

The user-level split is the correct basic choice because clips from the same participant are strongly dependent. A random video-level split would cause clear identity and behavioral leakage.

However, the current protocol could be strengthened:

- With only 331 users, the nominal 7:1:2 split gives roughly 232/33/66 users per split. Report **user counts**, not only video counts.
- Random grouped splits can produce substantial variation because users contribute 1–17 clips. Consider grouped five-fold cross-validation or balancing splits by both user count and number of clips.
- Hyperparameter selection, standardization, percentile construction, prompt calibration, and any learned fusion weights must be performed separately using only each seed’s training/validation users.
- The five random test sets overlap, so they should not be treated as five independent replications in significance tests.

### GPT-5.6 on all videos

Evaluating GPT-5.6 on all 2,011 videos is not a valid head-to-head comparison if the learned methods are evaluated only on held-out test videos.

It may be acceptable as a separate descriptive experiment only if:

1. GPT-5.6 is completely frozen;
2. It receives no labels, demonstrations, or calibration from the dataset;
3. Prompts were fixed without inspecting test performance; and
4. Results are explicitly labeled as non-comparable.

For the primary comparison, every method should be evaluated on exactly the same held-out users for each seed. Otherwise:

- Sample difficulty differs;
- Paired bootstrap testing is impossible;
- GPT’s evaluation includes users assigned to training and validation for other methods;
- Any prompt engineering based on the full dataset becomes a form of test-set adaptation.

A clean protocol would run GPT-5.6 and all other methods on each seed’s test split and retain per-video/per-user paired predictions.

## 3. Adequacy of MediaPipe and librosa for AAAI 2027

They are adequate as components of an engineering pipeline, but their use alone is not enough to establish a technically rigorous or novel AAAI contribution.

The largest concern is the claim that facial landmarks yield 14 AUs. MediaPipe Face Mesh does not directly provide validated FACS AU intensities. Hand-crafted landmark-to-AU rules must be:

- Fully specified or cited;
- Validated against a recognized AU detector or FACS-annotated benchmark;
- Evaluated for head-pose, illumination, occlusion, facial morphology, and demographic sensitivity.

Likewise, terms such as “confidence-associated patterns,” “fluidity,” “rigidity,” and “fist tightness” require operational definitions. Otherwise, they risk encoding unvalidated psychological assumptions.

For audio, librosa-derived pitch and RMS are reasonable basic features, but ten prosodic dimensions are limited. The paper should document:

- Voice activity detection;
- Pitch extraction algorithm and valid frequency ranges;
- Treatment of unvoiced frames;
- Noise and clipping handling;
- Speaker normalization;
- How speech rate is computed and which ASR system supplies words;
- Whether pauses distinguish silence from failed VAD;
- Missing-data handling.

Additional concerns include:

- The derivation of 1,260 facial dimensions from the listed measurements is unclear;
- The handling of variable-duration videos and 3-second windows is unspecified;
- The weighted gesture fusion layer needs a training procedure or fixed-weight rationale;
- Reliability and extraction failure rates should be reported;
- Feature normalization must be fitted on training data only.

For a competitive 2027 paper, I would expect comparisons against stronger pretrained representations, such as facial affect encoders, wav2vec/HuBERT-style speech embeddings, skeleton transformers, or video-language models. Interpretability can justify hand-crafted cues, but then the paper should validate cue accuracy and show that interpretability provides a measurable benefit.

## 4. Strength of the TF–IDF baseline

The TF–IDF baseline is too weak as the only text baseline.

It is useful as a classical lexical baseline, but it does not establish that behavioral cues outperform modern semantic representations. At minimum, I would add:

- Sentence/document embeddings followed by Ridge or an MLP;
- A pretrained transformer text regressor;
- A text-only LLM using the same prompting and scoring protocol as CueCoT;
- Transcript-only, summary-only, and transcript-plus-summary variants.

A same-LLM text-only control is particularly important. Otherwise, gains may come from the LLM’s prior knowledge or prompt format rather than from the proposed behavioral features.

Gemini-generated summaries also introduce confounds:

- Which Gemini version and decoding settings were used?
- Did summaries contain inferred personality or performance judgments?
- Were they generated from transcripts only or from video?
- Could the summarizer itself inject target-like assessments?

The raw transcript should be the primary text baseline; generated summaries should be an additional ablation. Stronger multimodal comparisons using transcript plus behavioral features would also be useful.

## 5. Counting the “14 baselines”

The stated count is ambiguous and does not match a natural enumeration.

### If every reported configuration is counted

- Unimodal/feature regressors:  
  3 algorithms × 4 feature sets = **12**
- Multimodal fusion methods: **4**
- LLM methods:  
  3 prompting methods × 2 LLM backends = **6**
- Text baseline: at least **1**, potentially **2** if transcript and summary are separate

This gives **23–24 configurations**, including CueCoT.

### Likely intended count

The authors may be counting method families:

- SVR, Ridge, RF: 3
- Four multimodal fusion methods: 4
- Three LLM prompting methods on two models: 6
- TF–IDF: 1

Total: **3 + 4 + 6 + 1 = 14**.

However, this count includes the two CueCoT implementations as “methods” even though CueCoT is the proposed approach, not a baseline. It also counts LLM backends separately while collapsing four feature configurations for each classical regressor. That counting rule is inconsistent.

The paper should distinguish clearly among:

- **Baseline method families**;
- **Backbone variants**;
- **Feature-set ablations**;
- **Proposed-method variants**.

In terms of relevance, the classical regressors are useful diagnostics, and CRMF and Attention-LSTM appear to be meaningful comparisons. Direct prompting and generic CoT are necessary ablations. Nevertheless, the suite is not fully comprehensive because it lacks strong pretrained text, audio, video, and multimodal representation baselines. Counting many SVR/Ridge/RF feature variants does not substitute for contemporary baselines.

## 6. Bootstrap confidence intervals and Cohen’s \(d\)

The general idea is appropriate, but the resampling unit is critical.

Because each participant contributes multiple videos, ordinary video-level bootstrap resampling violates independence and will produce overly narrow confidence intervals. The bootstrap should be **clustered by user**:

1. Resample held-out users with replacement;
2. Include all clips for each sampled user;
3. Recompute the complete macro-averaged metric;
4. For method comparisons, bootstrap the paired predictions from the same sampled users.

Additional recommendations:

- Report confidence intervals for the **difference between methods**, not only for each method separately.
- Use the same held-out examples for paired tests.
- Correct for multiple comparisons if significance is claimed across many baselines, metrics, and 12 targets.
- Explain whether macro-averaging occurs before or after bootstrapping.
- For correlations, clustered bootstrap CIs are reasonable; a Fisher transformation is another option but does not itself solve within-user dependence.
- Since test sets overlap across the five random seeds, do not pool seed-level results as independent observations.
- A paired Cohen’s \(d\) should be defined on user-level paired losses or another clearly specified independent unit. Computing \(d\) from only five seed-level values is unstable, while computing it over videos ignores clustering.
- Correlation effect sizes are not naturally summarized by Cohen’s \(d\); report changes in \(r\)/\(\rho\) and their clustered CIs directly.

A further issue is that MSE and MAE depend on label scaling. The paper should state whether target normalization was predetermined by the dataset or recomputed within each split. Any split-dependent transformation must be fitted on the training set only.

## 7. Experimental rigor rating

**Current rating: 5/10.**

### Strengths

- User-level splitting addresses the most obvious leakage risk.
- Multiple random seeds are better than a single split.
- Several metrics are reported.
- Direct and generic-CoT variants provide relevant prompting ablations.
- Cross-backbone LLM evaluation is potentially useful.
- The proposed behavioral features are interpretable in principle.

### Major weaknesses

- The soft-prompt description contradicts the actual feature serialization.
- GPT evaluation on all videos is not directly comparable to split-based evaluation.
- The AU and emotion extraction claims are insufficiently validated.
- Several feature definitions and dimensionalities are not reproducible from the description.
- The text baseline is substantially weaker than contemporary alternatives.
- The “14 baselines” count is inconsistent.
- The statistical protocol does not state that resampling is clustered by participant.
- Repeated random splits are overlapping rather than independent.
- Modern pretrained modality and multimodal baselines are missing.
- Potential leakage through global percentile estimation, normalization, prompt development, and generated summaries is not addressed.

With same-test-set evaluation for all methods, participant-clustered inference, stronger modern baselines, training-only preprocessing, and validation of the handcrafted behavioral cues, the rigor could plausibly rise to **7–8/10**. In its current form, the experimental conclusions appear promising but are not yet sufficiently controlled for a strong AAAI empirical claim.

## 3. Is the “first to apply CoT to behavioral assessment” claim justified?

**No—not in its present form.**

“Behavioral assessment” is extremely broad and can include:

- emotion recognition,
- mental-health assessment,
- personality inference,
- social-interaction analysis,
- clinical behavioral coding,
- human-activity understanding,
- educational and workplace assessment,
- deception or intent analysis.

It is unlikely that a blanket priority claim across all these areas can be defended. In addition, “structured CoT reasoning” needs a precise definition. Prompting a model to produce cue descriptions followed by a prediction may be better described as **structured rationalization** or **rationale generation**, unless the method actually uses intermediate reasoning steps in training or inference.

A safer claim would be:

> To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate structured language rationales for multimodal reasoning over nonverbal cues in asynchronous video-interview assessment.

Even this should be supported by:

1. A documented literature search;
2. Careful delimitation to **asynchronous video interviews**;
3. Comparison with prior explainable interview-assessment systems;
4. A distinction between **human-readable rationales** and **faithful explanations**.

I would also revise:

> use reasoning transparency as an analytical tool

to something like:

> use generated structured rationales as an analytical probe

unless you independently test rationale faithfulness. “Transparency” implies access to the true basis of the model’s decision, which ordinary CoT generation does not guarantee.

Likewise, the statement that nonverbal-signal reliability “has rarely been tested empirically” is too strong. Nonverbal cues and interview judgments have been studied extensively in psychology. Your more defensible novelty may be testing whether **the specific machine-extracted cues used by automated interview systems contain stable predictive information under your dataset and evaluation design**.

ROUND 4: Discussion, Ethics & Final Verdict
============================================================
**WEAK REJECT.** The paper is reasonably transparent about the failed CoT approach, and the cross-model agreement and feature-shuffling controls make the negative result potentially valuable rather than merely post hoc. However, the central reframing—that behavioral signals are fundamentally weak or that the field’s behavioral-signal assumption is wrong—is not justified by evidence from a single dataset; the results establish failure for these features, representations, and ratings, not a general information limitation. The proposed explanations remain largely speculative without evaluator studies, transcript-controlled analyses, or direct human evaluation, leaving some tension between the broad framing and the narrower empirical findings. The ethics discussion is directionally appropriate, but should address dataset bias, demographic subgroup effects, construct validity, and risks arising from content-based assessment as well as behavior-based systems.
